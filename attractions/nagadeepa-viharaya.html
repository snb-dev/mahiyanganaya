Protected ancient temple

Nagadeepa Viharaya

An Uraniya heritage site near Mahiyanganaya with stupa ruins, early inscriptions and folklore linked to King Dutugemunu.

Overview

Nagadeepa Viharaya at Uraniya, also listed as Nagadeepa Raja Maha Vihara, Uraneeya, is an ancient Buddhist temple site in the Rideemaliyadda area of Badulla District. It is separate from the famous Nagadeepa Purana Vihara on Nainativu in Jaffna, so visitors planning a Mahiyanganaya trip should search for Uraniya or Rideemaliyadda when checking directions.

History

Uva tourism sources describe the site as an old temple complex connected in local folklore to King Dutugemunu, who is said to have used the area as a camp and training ground before the campaign against King Elara. The same source notes that the site is associated in village belief with ancient Naga traditions. The archaeological evidence mentioned for the temple includes inscriptions from around the 6th to 10th centuries AD, showing that the site remained important across several periods.

What to See

The visible remains are described as two stupas and the foundations or ruins of several buildings. One smaller stupa is reported to survive up to the Devatha Kotuwa, while a larger stupa now appears as an overgrown mound with an old image house on top. Look for stonework, inscription stones and the quiet forest-edge setting, but treat the ruins carefully and avoid climbing or moving anything.

Planning Notes

Published visitor information is still limited, so this is best treated as a quiet heritage stop rather than a fully serviced attraction. Confirm road access locally before setting out, dress modestly, carry water, and allow extra time if visiting after rain. Pair it with Mahiyangana Raja Maha Viharaya, Sorabora Wewa or another Rideemaliyadda-side heritage stop if you are building a slower cultural route.

Responsible Visit

The site is listed as a protected archaeological monument, with the protected-monuments record naming Nagadeepa Raja Maha Vihara at Uraneeya in Rideemaliyadda and giving 18 November 1960 as its registration date. Keep the visit low-impact: stay on existing paths, do not climb the stupas or image-house remains, avoid flash photography around worship areas, and ask before photographing monks or residents.
